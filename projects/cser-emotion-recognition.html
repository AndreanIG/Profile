CSER: Context-Sensitive Emotion Recognition Framework

A multimodal system that fuses Vision Transformers and Vision-Language Models to understand human emotion through context, not just faces.

Overview

Most emotion recognition models rely on a single modality such as facial expressions or voice. But human emotion is shaped by context: the surrounding scene, body posture, and situation all matter. CSER closes that gap by making emotion recognition context-aware, using visual and textual features together.

This research was written with Dr. Thuseethan Selvarajah, Prof. Sutharshan Rajasegarar, and Prof. John Yearwood, and submitted to IEEE Transactions on Multimedia.

Technical Summary

CSER combines three ViT-based visual learners for face, body, and scene, plus textual embeddings from LLaVA and BLIP. All modalities are projected into a shared latent space and fused with multi-head and self-attention mechanisms.

  • Facial region analysis with a Vision Transformer.
  • Body posture and scene context with separate ViT learners.
  • Textual scene descriptions generated and embedded by LLaVA and BLIP.
  • Cross-modal fusion in a shared latent space for final emotion prediction.

The Hard Part

  • Dataset scarcity: group and context-sensitive emotion datasets were nearly impossible to find. EMOTIC and HECO were the only usable options, both with fragmented labels and inconsistent annotations that required remapping.
  • Data quality: many images had occluded faces, low light, or incomplete subjects. I built fallback logic that replaced missing faces with zero matrices to keep the training pipeline stable.
  • Hardware constraints: all experiments started on a GTX 1660 Ti, forcing constant trade-offs between batch size and learning rate. I used Deakin's research GPU infrastructure and Hugging Face compute credits to run full-scale experiments.
  • VLM integration: LLaVA and BLIP produced embeddings with incompatible dimensions and token structures. Aligning and projecting them into one feature space was one of the hardest implementation challenges.

Reflection

As the youngest member of the author list, I handled most of the experimental setup, ablation design, and manuscript drafting. This taught me to think beyond implementation: questioning design decisions, justifying model behaviour, and communicating results professionally. Scientific progress is not just about accuracy, it is about the discipline to keep iterating when nothing seems to work.
